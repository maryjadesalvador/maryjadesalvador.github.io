Introduction

This portfolio presents my professional journey, learning experiences, and growth as an educator through the CLAD Through CTEL program. It highlights the knowledge and teaching strategies I have gained to better support English Learners and create an inclusive learning environment.

I have nine years of teaching experience, including eight years in the Philippines and my current year as a J-1 exchange teacher in the United States. I earned my Bachelor’s degree in Education from Colegio de San Juan de Letran and completed my master’s degree in the Philippines. My educational background and teaching experience have helped me develop a strong commitment to providing quality education and supporting students with diverse learning needs.

As a new teacher in the United States, I have encountered new experiences and challenges, particularly in teaching students from different cultural and linguistic backgrounds. The California Teachers of English Learners (CTEL) program, which leads to the California English Learner Authorization (CLAD), has helped me develop a deeper understanding of English language development and effective instructional strategies. Through this program, I have gained valuable knowledge that I can apply to make lessons more accessible, encourage student participation, and create an inclusive learning environment.

I believe that professional development in English Learner education is essential for educators because it equips them with the knowledge and skills to address students’ language and academic needs. The program has strengthened my teaching practices and inspired me to continue growing professionally to ensure that all students receive meaningful learning opportunities and the support they need to succeed.

Discussion 2

BICS and CALP in My Science Classroom

Cummins’ distinction reminds me not to equate conversational fluency with academic readiness. Visuals, sentence frames, and structured talk build academic language while home languages stay valued as assets.

Read the full response

One linguistic theory that will help me better serve linguistically and culturally diverse students is Jim Cummins' distinction between Basic Interpersonal Communication Skills (BICS) and Cognitive Academic Language Proficiency (CALP). Cummins explains that English learners often develop conversational English within a few years, while academic English, the language needed for reading, writing, and understanding complex classroom content, can take five to seven years or more to develop (Cummins, 2008). This reminds me not to equate conversational fluency with academic readiness.

In my middle school science classroom, this theory encourages me to provide language supports such as visuals, sentence frames, graphic organizers, and structured discussions while maintaining high academic expectations. It also reinforces the importance of valuing students' home languages as assets that support learning. By recognizing the difference between social and academic language, I can create a more equitable learning environment where English learners can successfully develop both language proficiency and content knowledge.

Cummins, J. (2008). BICS and CALP: Empirical and theoretical status of the distinction. In B. Street & N. H. Hornberger (Eds.), Encyclopedia of language and education: Vol. 2. Literacy (2nd ed., pp. 71–83). Springer.

Final Project 4

Mini-Lesson Plan: Genetic Inheritance

A science lesson combining two strategies from The ELL Teacher’s Toolbox, Strip Story and Jigsaw, so students use reading, writing, speaking, and listening to explain how traits are inherited.

Lesson at a glance
  • Level: Intermediate English Learners (secondary)
  • Strip Story (15 min): students each hold one sentence about inheritance, then order them using first, next, then, and finally.
  • Jigsaw (30 min): expert groups master one of four sections (genes and chromosomes, dominant and recessive traits, Punnett squares, inherited traits in humans) and teach their home groups.
  • Exit ticket: 3–4 sentences using at least two sequence or cause-and-effect words, read aloud to a partner.

Foundations and Methods of English Language/Literacy Development and Content Instruction

Course 2 · 196284

Course description. This course focuses on research-based theories of how students learn a second language and develop reading and writing skills. It explores the differences between first and second language learning and the effects of cognitive, language, cultural, and emotional factors on students from diverse backgrounds. It also teaches how to use ELPAC or CELDT results to plan lessons that support English language development and academic success. In addition, the course introduces effective teaching strategies through English Language Development (ELD), Content-Based ELD, and Specially Designed Academic Instruction in English (SDAIE) to make lessons easier for English Learners to understand.

Assessment of English Learners

Course 3 · 198481

Course description. Assessment of English Learners focuses on how to assess and support English learners based on academic standards. It teaches how to identify students’ strengths and areas for improvement in English language development, literacy, and academic performance. Final Project

Lesson Plan

Differentiation and Formative Assessment: Kinetic and Potential Energy

A 55-minute 8th grade physical science lesson for Emerging, Expanding, and Bridging English Learners, with a ball drop and pendulum demonstration, three lab stations, an energy-transformation diagram, and a turn-and-talk. It is aligned to CA ELD Standards Parts I and II, with Bloom’s-level questions, leveled sentence frames, and three formative checks.

Lesson at a glance
  • Content objective: explain kinetic and potential energy, identify real-world examples, and describe how energy converts as height and speed change.
  • Language objective: describe the connection orally and visually using academic vocabulary and cause-and-effect sentence frames.
  • Differentiation: realia and picture cards, mixed-proficiency pairs, frames by level, and drawing, gestures, or short spoken answers as ways to show learning.
  • Formative assessment: quick-draw whiteboard check, turn-and-talk with sentence frames, and an exit ticket sorted into “got it,” “getting there,” and “not yet.”
“When the ball is at the top, it has ______ energy because ______.”

Reflective Essay

Essay

Culture and Education

My perspective: culture and education are inseparable; engagement rises when lessons connect to students’ backgrounds. New insight: equity is not identical treatment. It means providing the tools and opportunities each student needs, and culturally responsive teaching means including students’ cultures intentionally while keeping expectations high. Changes I will make: learn more about students’ cultures, keep high expectations with scaffolds, examine my own biases, engage families, and add collaborative learning.

Final Reflection and Conclusion

Putting this portfolio together made me look back at how much I've changed as a teacher. When I started the program, I figured helping English Learners mostly meant teaching them vocabulary. I see it differently now. It also means paying attention to culture, identity, and whether students feel safe enough to speak up in class. Giving every student the same lesson isn't the same as giving every student a fair chance, and I didn't really get that before.

The lesson that stuck with me most is that a student can talk easily with friends and still struggle with academic language. Think of a kid who chats nonstop on the playground but can't follow multi-step directions or explain their thinking in class. I can't judge what a student needs just by hearing how they talk during recess. Pictures, graphic organizers, sentence frames, and group work help with this. They don't water down the lesson. They let students do the same learning as everyone else while their English catches up.

I also learned to stop treating home languages and cultures as problems to work around. When students feel their background is respected, they take more risks and share more. Their first language can help them make sense of new ideas, and their experiences can make class discussions better. That changed how I see my job. I'm not only teaching a language. I'm helping students feel good about who they are.

The Culture and Inclusion course and the Assessment of English Learners course taught me to respect differences and use test results to plan lessons. One score never tells the full story. A student might be strong in speaking and listening but still developing in writing, and I need to see that. Instead of focusing on what students can't do yet, I want to notice what they already do well and build from there. Assessment is only useful if it changes how I teach.

Every student learns differently, and this program made me more flexible and patient when I plan. Now I ask myself, "Who might struggle with this, and what can I change?" In PE, that means giving clear directions, demonstrating activities, using visual cues, and pairing students so they can help each other. PE is actually a great place for language learning because students can see, move, and practice together. I can teach words like "pass," "balance," and "aim" while students use them in real action, and teamwork gives them natural reasons to talk and make friends.

Overall, this program made me want to do better for all my students. Supporting English Learners takes ongoing learning and reflection, and I know I won't get everything right. I'll keep learning from my students, my coworkers, and my mistakes. My goal is a classroom where students from every language and cultural background feel welcome, valued, and confident that they can succeed.
